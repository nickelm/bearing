## Vision

Bearing is a late-Cold-War air war on a procedurally generated, Earth-like planet. Two fictional blocs, the **Alliance** and the **Pact**, fight across whatever geography the seed produces: a single supercontinent, two continents divided by an ocean, or a scattered archipelago. Fourth-generation fighters, strike aircraft, attack helicopters, bombers, AWACS, tankers, and transports fly over a ground and naval war that runs under AI control.

The player flies. The war runs itself. At any moment the player can search the ongoing missions of their side (a CAP over the front, a low-level interdiction strike, an attack helicopter CAS sortie, a combat airdrop) and jump into any aircraft involved. When the player leaves, the AI takes the aircraft back and the mission continues. The tone we're aiming for is *Red Storm Rising*: a theater-spanning war in which air power decides whether fronts hold and convoys get through.

Bearing has no cockpits. The player flies every aircraft and helicopter from a third-person chase camera, with the aircraft near the center of the screen. A HUD overlay carries what the pilot needs while looking outside; multifunction displays docked at the screen edges carry the sensor pictures and configuration (radar, moving map, threat warning, stores), and a status strip below the aircraft carries discrete states. Master modes reconfigure all three together, as in fourth-generation fighters. The displays show only what that aircraft and its crew know. Following an AI flight and flying it use the same view: taking control changes who provides the inputs, and the screen stays the same.

The war is seen in two views of the same world. The **strategic view** is an illustrated globe in the spirit of Civilization VI and Cold War operational maps. Exaggerated relief, painted biomes, mountain and city glyphs, a road hierarchy, and labels for cities, ranges, peaks, rivers, and seas show at a glance where the front runs, which roads feed it, and how far the bombers must fly. It also shows day and night and the weather, because both shape every sortie. The **simulation view** is the faceted, low-poly world under soft cinematic light where the player flies, from the ground to about 20 km. The map is where the player plans and chooses; the simulation view is where they fly. A short transition carries the camera from one to the other.

## Design Pillars

1. **The world comes first.** Generation is unsteered. The campaign reads the map and adapts to it.
2. **The war runs without the player.** Missions are planned, flown, and resolved whether or not anyone watches.
3. **Any aircraft, any mission, either side.** The player chooses a side and then flies whatever interests them.
4. **Fidelity is a level of detail in space and time.** The simulation refines what the player looks at and coarsens what the player ignores.
5. **Plausible over clever.** The AI must produce a believable war, but it does not need to win one. The player's attention rests on flying.

## Decisions So Far

- **Name:** Bearing.
- **Planet:** Earth-like, about 1,000 km radius, generated from a seed with no steering toward a particular geography.
- **Campaign:** Detected from the generated world as a set of theaters; one world can contain several.
- **Casting:** Automatic and deterministic. Players get the war their seed produces and do not confirm or edit it.
- **Sides:** Alliance and Pact are both playable. The player picks a side at campaign start and keeps it for the campaign. The side is a view: the simulation never reads it.
- **Player role:** Pilot, not commander. The player does not command ground or naval forces. By default the player is a ghost who can take any aircraft of their side; they also have a named pilot in one squadron who builds reputation by flying its missions (proposal 0008).
- **Core loop:** Search ongoing missions, jump in, fly, hand back. No takeoff or landing by default, except in helicopters, whose missions end on the ground (decision 149). The war itself runs in a cycle of plan, execute, resolve, consequence and replan that never waits for the player (see *The Game Loop*).
- **Flight view:** A third-person chase camera and no cockpits. The HUD, docked displays and status strip are 2D overlays, and every item lives in exactly one of them (decision 189). AI pilots and the player share one flight director and one flight model; helicopters have their own rotor-disc model behind the same director interface (decision 149).
- **Ranges:** Aircraft ranges compressed by a factor that the casting step adapts to each world; speeds stay real.
- **Time:** Variable time acceleration from the first demo.
- **Day, night, seasons, and weather:** In demo scope. They shape air operations: night sorties, weather cancellations, seasonal snow.
- **Terrain-adaptive networks:** Rivers, roads and railways take their form from their design regime, and the terrain takes their earthworks as analytic edits, with river channels always cut last; every crossing has a bridge or culvert (decision 161).
- **World diversity:** Landform, biome and surface character are separate layers: terrain provinces and rock from tectonic history, biomes from a climate with local modifiers, surface from soil, water and disturbance. Continents have a character, regions are named zones with their own look, and each world has a handful of named natural wonders (decision 204).
- **Generation time:** No time budget. Generation may take minutes, and finished worlds are cached.
- **Hosting:** Static site on GitHub Pages. No server, no SharedArrayBuffer.
- **Campaign:** Two to four weeks of game time, ending in a breakthrough, a successful defense, or a ceasefire from mutual exhaustion (proposal 0008).
- **Persistence:** An event log of player inputs and campaign decisions from the tech demo on; saves in IndexedDB with file export and import.
- **Multiplayer:** Later. The event log keeps co-op possible.

## The Tech Demo

The first goal is one day of war (proposal 0008; `docs/roadmap.md`, phase R2): one continental-front theater, 24 game hours, four mission types (CAP, interdiction, CAS and airlift), three to five flyable aircraft with at least one per side, aggregate battalions with minimal entity promotion near the player, a survivor and a CSAR mission for every ejection, aggregate outcomes calibrated against full fidelity, and an event log that replays to the same state. The player finds a mission through search, follows it, takes over, flies it, and hands it back. The roadmap lists the exit criteria. The demo goals of revision 11, which left player flight control out of the demo, are superseded.

## The Game Loop

*Specified in `docs/proposals/0008-game-loop-and-campaign.md` (decision 228).*

The war runs in a cycle that never waits for the player:

1. **Plan.** Each side's command layer builds an air tasking order from theater needs, available aircraft, pilots and supplies, and revises it every few game hours.
2. **Execute.** Missions fly. Most resolve at the aggregate level; missions the player watches or flies run at full fidelity.
3. **Resolve.** Engagements produce losses, damage, delivered supplies, destroyed targets and ejected pilots.
4. **Consequence.** Results feed the ground war, the naval war, logistics and morale: fronts move, depots empty, bridges fall, squadrons lose pilots.
5. **Replan.** The next planning cycle responds to the new situation.

The player enters the loop through mission search (see *Mission Search and Jump-In*). A good sortie changes the war through the same consequence step as any other mission, amplified by morale and reputation. The campaign clock drives one order per game day with revisions every two to four hours; ground and naval aggregates step on fixed ticks; takeoffs, arrivals, engagements, ejections and phase changes are events (see *Time*).

- **Mission types are plug-ins.** Each has a planner rule with its priority score, a package template, an aggregate resolver, full-fidelity behavior for the AI pilots, jump-in windows, success criteria and consequences, a briefing template with named places, and interface defaults (master mode, formats and HUD cues per phase). A mission type is complete only when every part exists.
- **Aircraft types are bundles.** Each type has geometry with animated parts, flight data (fixed-wing or rotary), an avionics profile with its display complement, pylon stations and loadouts, role tags for the planner, crew roles, camera rig settings, aggregate performance values from calibration, and a faction, name and skin. All parts live in the type's JSON specification (decision 232); roadmap R1.7 moves the avionics profiles and the air-war entries there from code.
- **Fidelity consistency.** Aggregate resolvers and full fidelity must agree, or the war would behave differently when watched. Aggregate outcome tables are fitted offline from many headless full-fidelity engagements per matchup; the tables live in the repository, a script regenerates them, and tests keep the two within tolerance. Promotion builds full-fidelity state from the aggregate (positions, fuel, weapons, damage) and demotion folds it back; both preserve losses and expended weapons.
- **Aggregate resolution** (decision 250). At its phase events a flight that enters enemy airspace can meet an enemy CAP whose station lies within reach of its way in; escorts and defense suppression fight with it, and each side's losses follow the other's share of the forces, by the types' air values. Over its target it faces the target's air defense, halved when the package suppresses defenses. At egress, or when it turns home or is lost, its plug-in's resolver gives the outcome (damage, time on station, cargo delivered) and its consequences change the world: damage to sites, bridges and road edges, base stocks, attacks on troops for the ground war. A flight that loses half its aircraft turns home, and an unescorted strike flight that is engaged often jettisons and turns home. Lost aircraft lower the squadron's strength; each crew member ejects as a survivor or is killed, and leaves the roster. Until R2.10 fits them, each type's air, strike and survival values are a prior from its role and its radar, so better sensors count on either side.
- **Player identity.** As a ghost, the player can take any aircraft of their side without lasting consequence. The named pilot belongs to one squadron; flying its missions builds reputation, which lifts squadron morale and may draw the enemy's attention (raids on the home base, fighters hunting the callsign). A named pilot who is captured or killed loses the reputation, and the player creates a new one.
- **Ejection and CSAR.** Every ejection on either side creates a survivor with a position, an injury state, a survival timer and a capture risk that grows near enemy forces. The owning side always tasks a CSAR mission (rescue helicopter, escorts, on-scene cover), which ends with the survivor rescued, captured or killed. Rescued pilots return to their squadron's pool, which limits sortie generation; the player may fly any part of a CSAR mission or try to stop the enemy's.
- **Campaign.** Two to four weeks of game time, about 10 to 30 hours of play. Fuel, munitions, spares, replacement aircraft and pilots flow over the road, rail and sea networks, and base stocks gate sorties. Unit, squadron and theater morale change sortie rates, AI skill and ground combat. After a mission the map shows the chain of consequences (bridge down, supply cut, battalion withdrawing). The war ends in a breakthrough, a successful defense or a ceasefire from exhaustion, and a summary closes it.
- **Persistence.** The simulation records an event log of player inputs and campaign-level decisions. A save is a state snapshot plus the log since it; replaying the log from the snapshot reproduces the same state, which also serves replays, debugging and later co-op. The host keeps such snapshots as checkpoints every six game hours, so seeking back in time restores the latest one before the target and replays the player's inputs from there. Saves live in IndexedDB and export and import as files.

## Scale and Range

We keep a radius of about **1,000 km**, which gives a maximum surface distance of about 3,100 km and a surface area of about 12.6 million km².

Since geography now varies, a fixed range factor no longer fits every world. The casting step chooses a **range factor** between 0.35 and 1.0 per campaign, so that the theaters it detected remain playable. Two targets set it: a medium strike aircraft operating from rear bases must reach the full depth of the enemy side of a front, and a long-range bomber must reach across the widest contested sea lane. The factor scales the fuel load only, and burn rates stay real, so radius scales by the factor (decision 132). Speeds, climb rates, and weapon ranges stay real.

Landmasses are scaled to the planet; landforms are not (decision 121). Regional extents follow the landmasses: proposal 0007's zones, blends and variety windows are its Earth figures times R / R_Earth (decision 205). Elevations and depths at the scale of continents, ranges and plateaus are Earth-like reference values times R / R_Earth ≈ 0.157, so the mean ocean depth is about 600 m. Hills, valleys and ridges, the relief within about 20 km of a valley floor, have Earth-scale height (decisions 121, 122): erosion carves them in reference meters, and their height above the local valley floor is multiplied by a landform gain of 12 when converting to physical meters, so mountain valleys run a kilometer or more deep. Above a soft limit, land rises at 40% of the rate. Proposal 0007 gives the limit to the terrain province (young collision ranges 8–9 km, old mountains near 2.5 km); today one limit of 6.5 km serves every range, while provinces scale the uplift (young collision runs ×1.25, old mountains ×0.75), so the highest peaks reach about 10 km (decisions 210, 216). A limit per province is planned. Climate (lapse rate, snow, tree line) uses the Earth-like elevation: the valley floor in reference meters plus the height above it in physical meters. The atmosphere, aircraft performance, and service ceilings stay real. The strategic view exaggerates relief about 4× so ranges read on the map; the simulation view shows true scale.

World data is tiled. Processes that need the whole planet (erosion, drainage, climate, rivers, the front) run on a global grid of 1024 × 1024 cells per face, about 1.5 km per cell. Below that, tiles of 256 × 256 samples, addressed by (face, level, x, y), are synthesized on demand on the GPU as a pure function of direction, down to level 7 at about 48 m. Level-7 tiles over the ground-combat regions the casting step marks serve as the high-resolution regional bake. Tiles carve the landforms finer than the global grid from its climate and relief (decision 126): branching gullies and alpine crests in mountains, layered rock with benches, cliffs, buttes and mesas where horizontally layered sedimentary rock meets a dry climate (decisions 204, 210), skerry islands on glaciated coasts, reefs and lagoons on warm ones (decision 129), ragged shores in dry basins (decision 130), salt lakes and white playas in closed basins (decision 204; brine colors and pale shoreline rings are planned), cliffs on headlands, beaches in coves and marshes on flat, wet ground (decision 146), meanders on lowland rivers (migrating bends with cutoffs and oxbow lakes on large ones, inside their floodplains; decision 161), deltas at the mouths of large ones, green corridors along rivers through deserts, and falls where steep rivers cross the rock layers. Each river carries a water surface that never rises downstream, from the global cells it drains through, and the tiles cut its channel to it last (decision 146). Sand and gravel bars line the banks, and fast reaches run white (decision 147). Landforms that a height field cannot hold are instanced props placed by province and rock, with collision shapes for the flight model and line of sight (decision 204): tower karst, sandstone pillars and small inselbergs today (decision 225), collision in Free Flight's ground only; sea stacks and arches, hoodoos, natural arches and basalt columns are planned. Roads and railways are aligned to the tiles' relief with grades and curves by class; their embankments, cuttings and pads are analytic edits applied after the tiles' own detail, and river channels stay below every edit (decision 161).

## Technology

**TypeScript + Three.js with the WebGPU renderer and TSL shaders**, built with Vite and hosted as a static site on GitHub Pages. Compute shaders handle erosion, tile synthesis, and line-of-sight queries; world-generation kernels are plain WGSL on the render device, tested in Node on Dawn. Long-running CPU tasks run in Web Workers. Workers exchange state with `postMessage` and transferable buffers, since GitHub Pages cannot set the headers that SharedArrayBuffer requires. A Gamepad API binding drives the stick axes today; buttons and remapping come with roadmap R6.5.

## Architecture

The architecture separates **simulation** from **presentation**.

- **Simulation layer.** A headless model of the world, running in a worker that owns the game clock. It contains two parts: a **command layer** per side that plans missions against that side's intelligence picture, and an **execution layer** that moves and resolves everything (flights, formations, convoys, bases, weather).
- **Presentation layer.** Cameras, renderers, and UI that read simulation state. Two renderers draw the same world in different visual languages:
  - The **strategic view** (`src/render/strategic/`) is an orbit camera from about 3,000 km down to a zoom floor of about 100 km, over an illustrated base map with glyphs, labels, and military overlays.
  - The **simulation view** (`src/render/sim/`) covers 0 to about 20 km with chase, target, weapon, flyby, and observer cameras. It is entered only through a unit: following a flight or jumping into one of its aircraft, both in the same chase view, or an observer camera at a base or ship. It draws terrain only within the horizon: about 200 km at 20 km altitude, plus the distance at which the tallest terrain still shows above it (about 90 km for 4 km peaks at true scale).
  - The mission search panel sits over the strategic view. A transition controller (`src/render/common/`) eases the map camera down toward a flight and dissolves through cloud into the simulation view in under two seconds, and back. Both scenes stay resident.
- **World data.** The global grid's maps (height, biome, moisture, temperature, flow, forest density), tiles synthesized from them on demand, vector data (rivers, roads, railways, settlements, fronts, and the aligned corridors and structures of decision 161), terrain edits (pads, road and rail corridors, protected river channels) that one function applies to every height query, from the meshes to the flight model's ground, and the strategic graph. Campaign logic reads only the strategic graph; the execution layer may also query terrain.

Every unit exists at one of three fidelity levels:

- **Aggregate:** a formation, flight, or convoy with a position, strength, supply, and orders. Always simulated, cheaply.
- **Entity:** individual vehicles, aircraft, and infantry squads with positions, simple behaviors, and damage states. Simulated only inside a **promotion bubble** around the player's aircraft or the simulation view's camera.
- **Full:** physical simulation, used only for the aircraft the player controls or follows, with the flight model, sensor model, and flight director (see *Flying*). Full fidelity for campaign aircraft arrives with the tech demo (`docs/roadmap.md`, R2.5); until then a followed aircraft flies its analytic route, and only Free Flight's aircraft run at full fidelity.

Promotion expands an aggregate deterministically from its seed and current strength. Demotion folds entity losses back into aggregate strength, so a tank company the player destroyed stays destroyed.

Both renderers use **camera-relative rendering**: positions are float64 on the CPU, and we subtract the camera position before uploading to the GPU.

## Time

The simulation combines two clocks:

- **Discrete events** for anything with a predictable outcome. Flight and convoy positions follow analytically from their routes and departure times. Takeoffs, arrivals, mission phase changes, and engagement starts are scheduled events.
- **Fixed ticks** for processes that need iteration: aggregate ground combat (one tick per game minute), front movement, weather (one tick per game hour), the command layer's replanning (one order per game day, revised every two hours by the Alliance and every four by the Pact, with an hourly tick that commits the sorties about to depart; decision 249), and later the economy. Entity simulation inside the promotion bubble ticks at 10 to 30 Hz of game time.

Events and ticks share one priority queue ordered by game time, so their interleaving is exact. Under acceleration, the simulation processes more events and ticks per real second, so the state at a given game time is the same at 1×, 64×, and after a jump. Rates run from pause through 1×, 4×, 16× and 64× to 256×. Above about 16×, the promotion bubble collapses. At 256×, the view falls back to icons.

**Calendar and sun.** A 24-hour solar day, a 23.44° axial tilt, and a 365-day year. The sun direction is an analytic function of game time. The campaign opens in late summer in the main landmass's hemisphere and runs toward winter.

**Weather.** A synoptic model of lows and highs on a coarse cube grid (about 65 km), driven by the climate maps and the season, gives cloud cover and ceiling, visibility, precipitation from drizzle to the downpour of a storm core, thunderstorms, dust storms over arid land, temperature, and winds aloft. Flight operations and the sky on screen read the same fields. Free Flight runs the same model without the war, or a preset derived from it (clear, overcast, rain, storm, snow, fog, dust storm).

- **Auto-slowdown:** the simulation drops to 1× on events the player has subscribed to (see *Mission Search and Jump-In*).
- **Flight cap:** while the player controls an aircraft, acceleration is limited to cruise (up to 8×) and cancels on any threat. Cruise is in the air with the gear up, above about 1,000 ft, more than 40 km from the target or station; any enemy flight in the air within 120 km is a threat until the sensors read campaign contacts (R2.6). A rate above the cap drops to it, to 1× outside cruise (decision 274).

## Planet Parameters

| Parameter | Default | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Seed | random | Reproducible worlds |
| Radius | 1,000 km | Strategic depth |
| Plate count | 14 | Number and size of landmasses |
| Ocean fraction | 0.70 | Sea level, set by height percentile |
| Relief scale | 0.157 (R / R_Earth) | Elevations and depths of landmasses relative to Earth; landforms keep Earth's heights (decision 121) |
| Erosion strength | 1.0 | Erosion iterations and sediment capacity |
| Temperature offset | 0 °C | Ice age to hothouse |
| Moisture scale | 1.0 | Arid to lush |
| Axial tilt | 23.44° | Seasons |

The planet parameters shape the kind of war indirectly. Few plates with a low ocean fraction tend toward a supercontinent and a land war; many plates with a high ocean fraction tend toward archipelagos and an island campaign. The parameters never select a scenario directly.

## World Generation Pipeline

Each stage feeds the next, so features have causes.

1. **Cube-sphere grid.** Six cube faces projected onto the sphere with an equi-angular warp.
2. **Tectonic plates.** Seed points spread over the whole sphere grow into plates by flood fill, with types and drift vectors drawn from `rng("plates")` and no steering. Continental crust follows the continental plates, with coasts inside them. Convergent continental boundaries raise mountain ranges, convergent oceanic boundaries form trenches and island arcs, and divergent boundaries form rifts and mid-ocean ridges.
   - **Terrain provinces** (decision 204). From `rng("provinces")`, each boundary run gets an age (young or old), old continental plates get fossil sutures, and a few hotspots leave tracks along their plate's motion. Every land cell gets one of twelve provinces: young collision range, high plateau, old mountains, shield, basin and range, rift, volcanic arc, hotspot, sedimentary basin, karst platform, soft sediment, glaciated lowland. Provinces set the uplift profile (a soft limit per province is planned), the rock, and which landforms the tiles carve. Each continent, the continental crust of one plate (decision 212), gets a **character** (old shield, young mountains, dry extension, tropical platform and others) drawn from what its tectonics allow and its climate belt; it biases provinces and climate within those limits. If the two largest continents come out too similar, the smaller one's character is redrawn, at most twice, from a seeded sub-stream.
   - **Lithology** (decision 204). From `rng("lithology")`, rock by province with noise and layering: granite and gneiss, schist, sandstone, shale, limestone, basalt, volcanic ash, loess, glacial till. Rock sets erodibility and talus for erosion, soil for biomes, rock colors for shading, and gates landforms: mesas, buttes and stepped canyon walls only in layered sedimentary rock in dry climates, karst only in limestone.
3. **Detail noise.** Ridged multifractal noise amplified by uplift, plus domain warping on coastlines and ridges, on the global grid.
4. **Hydraulic and thermal erosion.** A GPU stream-power pass on the global grid carves drainage networks and canyons and lays down alluvial plains; a talus-angle pass handles thermal erosion. Where an ice age would have reached (an Earth-like annual mean below about 3 °C, spread down the valleys), valleys become U-shaped troughs with overdeepened floors, which hold long lakes behind the old ice fronts (decision 126). Erodibility and the talus angle follow the rock; troughs that reach the sea become fjords (decisions 204, 210); sharper arêtes and cirques are planned.
5. **Climate and biomes.** Temperature from latitude and altitude with a seasonal range from the axial tilt; moisture from prevailing winds with rain shadows; local modifiers for continentality, cold currents with fog deserts, precipitation seasonality, stronger orographic effects and aspect (decision 204; aspect is planned); twenty land biomes (tropical rainforest, cloud forest, monsoon forest, mangrove, savanna, sand sea, rocky desert, red rock desert, fog desert, cold desert and steppe, Mediterranean scrub, temperate grassland, temperate deciduous forest, temperate rainforest, moorland and heath, wetland, boreal forest, tundra, alpine meadow, ice) stacked by altitude on mountains, with soil from the rock, riparian corridors, interfingering ecotones, and (planned) oases and disturbance patches; forest density and tree line. Farmland stays a land-use overlay.
6. **Rivers.** Drainage recomputed exactly on the CPU after erosion (priority flood, D8), with flow accumulation thresholded into a river network with widths, and lakes. The polylines through cell centers are smoothed in plan (a Gaussian over about a cell, then corner cutting, with confluences and mouths held in place) so they leave the grid's axes (decision 165), and every river wanders gently over a few kilometers, less on steep reaches (decision 167). Each river runs on upstream from its source as a stream along its largest tributary, so rivers start high in the mountains (decision 177). Rivers then take a planform by regime: nearly straight mountain streams, migrating meanders with cutoffs and oxbow lakes on large lowland rivers inside their floodplains (Howard–Knutson migration, run once per world in the worker; decision 173) on a floodplain that a floodplain edit levels, procedural meanders on small streams, and distributaries in deltas. Each carries a water surface that never rises downstream (decisions 146, 161). Depressions fill only to the level where evaporation meets inflow: below the spill level a basin is closed (endorheic), its rivers end at a salt lake, or at a playa when nothing remains (decision 204). Open lakes under about 14 km², the erosion pits of a cell or two, silt up to their spill level, so rivers cross flat floors instead of strings of round ponds (decision 220).
7. **Feature extraction.** The maps become named-feature records, each with geometry (point, polyline, or polygon), extent, and an importance score: continents, oceans, seas, bays, straits, islands and archipelagos, mountain ranges (connected high-uplift ridge regions), peaks (local maxima by prominence), hills, deserts, major forests, wetlands, plains and plateaus, river main stems, and lakes. Polygons are rasters on the coarse grid with a label axis; oceans, seas and bays come from a watershed on the distance to land, so a constriction separates them (decision 95). Extraction runs inside the graph builder, after the land nodes and sea lanes. It also records salt flats and lists natural-wonder candidates where their conditions hold (decision 204). **Zones** of similar province and biome, 16–63 km across (the proposal's 100–400 km scaled to the planet, decision 205), with boundaries on ridges, rivers, coasts and escarpments where they exist and a look recipe each, are segmented on the eroded global grid after the global pass: they are presentation data, which campaign logic never reads.
8. **Naming.** A toponymy generator from `rng("names")` builds names from syllable inventories and phonotactic rules, with one naming flavor per cultural region, so neighboring regions' names sound different without copying real languages. For now a naming region is a land node: landmasses cycle through four flavors, archipelagos take a fifth, and water takes the flavor of the nearest land (decision 96). Generic terms complete each name: "the Varden Range", "Mount Kessar" (labeled with its height, "Mount Kessar 1,210 m"), "the Oskel Waste", "Lake Brann", "the Teller Strait". Settlements and invasion corridors ("the Kessar Gap") are named here too, and land nodes take the names of their continents, islands, and archipelagos. Zones and wonders draw from their own forks, `names/zones` and `names/wonders` ("the Varden Highlands", decision 204).
9. **Strategic graph.** See *Reading the World*. It includes the civilian geography and its hierarchy: settlements with urban extents, roads in classes (motorway, trunk, secondary) from a gravity model of the populations they connect and the traffic routed over them, railways linking major cities, ports, and industry, and bridges. Crossing sites on the rivers (narrow, straight reaches with firm banks) pull settlements toward them and set where roads cross (decision 175). Bridges stand at every crossing of a river that needs one (as network nodes that a strike can cut, planned; today each records the polyline it carries), and viaducts where a railway or major road crosses a deep valley; they are typed (beam, truss, arch, viaduct, suspension), carry their span, the traffic they carry and the detour if they fall, are all targets (decision 163), and are named after their river, town or feature ("Oskel Viaduct"); names for rail lines and notable river reaches are planned (decision 161). Invasion corridors get names (in the tradition of the Fulda Gap), major roads get route numbers ("R4", "M12"), and notable roads through passes or along coasts get names ("the Kessar Pass Road").
10. **Theater detection and casting.** See *Theaters* and *Casting*.
11. **Validity check.** The world must hold at least 15 airbase sites in each side's territory and at least one theater besides the strategic one. The per-side count needs the cast, so the check runs after casting. Worlds that fail derive a new sub-seed and regenerate, up to eight times. The retry sequence is seeded, so each seed still maps to exactly one world.
12. **Infrastructure from the cast:** military and commercial ports, airbases, and strategic targets (factories, refineries, depots, rail yards). Airbases are campaign facts on the coarse grid; once the eroded world exists, each runway is sited within about 6 km of its coarse point (15 km where nothing nearer grades fairly) on dry ground that needs the least grading, and that sited runway is what the simulation view draws and aircraft fly from (decision 220).
13. **Alignment** (decision 161), on the eroded world after the global pass: each road and railway of the graph is aligned between fixed anchors by one router with parameters per class (maximum grade, minimum radius, earthworks against viaducts and tunnels). Links are aligned independently in a spatial order by a pool of workers when the world is built, and stored with it (decision 184); aligning railways first and then roads, with earlier corridors as obstacles, is planned. The router reads the level-6 tiles' relief and lets switchbacks and horseshoe curves emerge; spiral templates for railways that cannot otherwise meet their grade are planned. Each alignment becomes a plan of straights and arcs, a profile with vertical curves, and corridor edits with banks, with a structure code per point and a record for every river crossing (records for viaducts and tunnels are planned). Network bridges are matched to crossings of the rendered river within 25 km; on seed 1983 fewer than half match (decision 190), which the roadmap settles. Campaign logic never reads this step.

Feature extraction, naming, infrastructure and alignment stay headless. Label placement and glyph instancing belong to the strategic renderer.

A coarse pass at 256 × 256 per face runs steps 1 to 12 on the CPU in the worker, without erosion, in about 4 to 6 seconds per world. Campaign facts come from this pass, so they are the same on every machine. The pass takes about 9–12 s per world since track W (2026-11-18 audit). The full pass (erosion and the eroded global maps) then runs on the GPU for the seed that passed. Tiles are synthesized on demand from the global maps (see *Scale and Range*). We cache each world, with its graph and cast, in IndexedDB; tiles are cheaper to regenerate than to load.

## Reading the World: The Strategic Graph

The strategic graph abstracts the generated world into the terms a commander uses. Theater detection, casting, and the command layer all query the graph; none of them read the heightmap directly.

- **Landmass nodes.** Every landmass above about 2,000 km². Smaller islands within strike range of each other merge into **archipelago nodes**. Attributes: area, centroid, terrain mix, coastline length, candidate port sites, airbase capacity (flat, low land), and the best barrier lines across it.
- **Sea lane edges.** Between pairs of landmass nodes, a least-cost route over a coarse ocean grid from port site to port site, with its length and the minimum width of water along it.
- **Chokepoints.** Places where a sea lane passes through a narrow strait (under about 150 km) or within about 300 km of an island that could host an airfield. Each chokepoint records which sea lanes it controls.
- **Barrier lines.** For each large landmass, the coast-to-coast lines of high barrier strength (rivers, ridges, forest) found by the front generator described below.
- **Civilian geography.** Settlements with population (city, town, village), habitability and urban extent polygons; the road network in classes (motorway, trunk, secondary) from a gravity model, with numbered routes and named pass and coast roads; railways with their stations, bridges and rail yards; and bridges where roads and railways cross rivers and deep valleys, with type, span, traffic and detour, all of them targets (decisions 161, 163). Settlements, roads and railways are world facts placed before casting, so casting and invasion corridors can use them. Each side's capital, its largest settlement, follows from the cast (decision 103).
- **Salt flats and wonders** (decision 204). Salt flats, which are also natural airstrip sites; natural-wonder candidates, from which casting selects 5 to 15 per world. Zones are not in the graph: they come from the eroded world (decision 205).
- **Named features.** The named geography of pipeline steps 7 and 8 joins the graph as a feature list (kind, anchor, geometry, extent, importance, name) with raster layers for its polygons, so briefings and mission names can use it ("interdiction strike on the Kessar Pass Road"). Each land node points to its feature, and each corridor carries its name (decisions 95, 96).

A debug overlay draws the graph on the globe. It is the most important tool for judging whether theater detection makes sense. The strategic view's military overlays draw the same graph for the player.

## Theaters

A theater is a module with a precondition on the strategic graph, a fit score, and a set of things it instantiates. One world usually supports several theaters, and the campaign links them through logistics flows: a cut sea lane starves a continental front, and a lost chokepoint island exposes the convoys behind it.

| Theater | Detected when | Instantiates |
|---|---|---|
| **Continental front** | A landmass large enough to split along a barrier line with both sides holding at least 35% of its area | Start line, defensive lines, invasion corridors, ground formations, dense airbases behind the front |
| **Sea lane** | A landmass that can serve as a rear base, connected to a continental front by a sea lane of 800 to 3,000 km | Convoy routes, ports, maritime patrol, anti-shipping strikes, convoy escort |
| **Chokepoint** | A chokepoint on an active sea lane | Island garrison, forward airfield, seizure and defense operations |
| **Island campaign** | Several archipelago or small landmass nodes within mutual strike range and no landmass large enough for a front | Island airfields, carrier groups, amphibious groups, island assaults |
| **Strategic** | Always, once both sides have industry | Industrial and logistics targets, air defense belts, strategic bomber missions |

Two examples show how this plays out. A two-continent world with an island chain between them yields a continental front, a sea lane, a chokepoint, and the strategic theater: the full *Red Storm Rising* war. A supercontinent yields a continental front and the strategic theater only, a Central Front war with deep strikes into the interior. An archipelago world yields an island campaign and the strategic theater, a war of carriers and island airfields that plays more like the Falklands or the Pacific.

### Automatic Front Line

The continental front theater uses the front generator. For each land cell, we compute barrier strength from river width, slope, ridge prominence, and forest density. A constrained shortest-path search finds coast-to-coast paths that maximize barrier strength while splitting the landmass within the required area ratio, repeated with exclusion corridors to yield three or four roughly parallel defensive lines. The start line sits one line forward of the strongest barrier, so the defender starts exposed and has a strong position to fall back to. Weak, well-roaded segments of each line become invasion corridors.

## Casting

Casting turns the detected theaters into a campaign. It draws from `rng("casting")` and never asks the player.

1. **Select theaters.** Keep every theater above its fit threshold.
2. **Assign territory.** The Pact receives mass and interior lines: the larger side of each front, the landmass farthest from the sea lanes, the larger island cluster. The Alliance receives the side adjacent to its rear base and its sea lanes. In island campaigns the Alliance holds a dispersed island network and the Pact assembles the armada.
3. **Set posture.** By default the Pact attacks and the Alliance defends in every theater.
4. **Set the range factor** as described in *Scale and Range*.
5. **Mark industry, port, base, and ground-combat regions** for each side, consistent with its territory and theaters. Industry regions sit on each side's largest urban clusters. Infrastructure placement (pipeline step 12) then puts the sites inside these regions.
6. **Select the natural wonders** (decision 204). From the graph's candidates, 5 to 15, favoring those near fronts and sea lanes, drawn from `rng("wonders")` so casting's own stream is unchanged. The tiles shape the tepuis, calderas, monoliths and blue holes (decision 221), props crowd the karst bays and pillar forests (decision 225), and the map labels them; keeping the alignment's roads and railways out of them is planned.
7. **Write the briefing.** A generated summary of the war the world produced: the theaters, the stakes in each, the first expected moves, and the wonders that serve as landmarks.

Casting runs before the player picks a side, and the cast does not depend on that choice. The same seed always produces the same war, and it unfolds identically until the player intervenes.

The table contains two deliberate asymmetries. The A-6 analogue flies only from Alliance carriers, so it appears wherever the cast contains carrier groups. The F-117 analogue is a rare late-war asset with a small fleet, flying single-ship night strikes against the most heavily defended targets. The Pact has no counterpart and relies on mass and SEAD instead. Stealth requires a radar cross-section per aircraft type in the air defense model, which every aircraft gets anyway.

The two sides also differ in doctrine. Pact flights depend more on ground-controlled interception and fly in larger, more tightly controlled formations. Alliance flights rely on AWACS and have more autonomy. These differences live in the command layer and in mission templates, so both sides use the same simulation code.

## Air Tasking

Each side's command layer produces an **air tasking order** (ATO) for each game day and revises it every few game hours. The planner is rule-based and deliberately simple:

1. **Collect needs** from each theater: front segments under pressure need CAS, high-value assets need CAP, enemy logistics and air defenses need strikes, bases low on stock need resupply, convoys need escort or attack.
2. **Rank needs** by a priority score that combines theater weights, target value, and urgency.
3. **Build packages.** Assign flights from squadrons within range, adding escort and SEAD to strikes when the target area is defended.
4. **Schedule** missions so that the result matches the doctrine of the side: Pact strikes in large waves, Alliance missions spread more evenly.

Each mission type contributes its planner rule and package template as a plug-in (see *The Game Loop*); the ATO asks every registered plug-in for its needs (decision 249, which retired decision 132's interim tasker). Needs are either **standing stations** (CAP, AEW and tanker orbits), kept covered by relieving flights to the order's horizon, or **wanted times on target** (CAS, strikes, airlift), laid out by doctrine through the day's light. Each side **apportions** its airborne aircraft between counter-air, support, strike, ground support and airlift, with shares that overlap, so no category's high scores can take the whole sky. An order holds planned sorties; each is committed to the war, and its aircraft reserved, about an hour before it departs, after a last check of its weather and squadron. A revision keeps what still stands, cancels with a reason what does not (the need is gone, the weather fell below minimums, the aircraft are lost), and plans what is missing. Until the ground war (roadmap R2.3), a front segment's pressure comes from the corridors that cross it; interdiction ranks bridges, depots, rail yards and road segments by how much of the enemy's supply to the front each carries over the road and rail graphs.

The planner never searches or learns. It must produce a plausible war, and its decisions must be legible to a player browsing missions: each mission records the need it serves and its score. Ties break by stable ids, and random draws come from per-side, per-day forks.

- **Known targets only.** Each side plans against its intelligence picture (see *Intelligence*), not against ground truth. Fixed installations are known from the outbreak; everything that moves must be found first.
- **Weather and daylight.** Missions are cancelled below their weather minimums, winds aloft change ground speeds, and low-level strikes favor night and bad weather. Minimums are each plug-in's data: daylight (CAS, interdiction and reconnaissance fly by day), a ceiling and visibility at the target, and the same base minimums for every takeoff. The planner forecasts by persistence (the weather when it plans), and commitment checks the weather of its hour.
- **Carriers.** A carrier group is a mobile base on an analytic sea route. Its air wing flies ATO missions, launching and recovering at the group's predicted position, with no deck operations.

## Intelligence

Each side keeps an intelligence picture of contacts (position, type, time last seen, confidence, source), and its confidence decays with age, fastest for moving units. Fixed installations (airbases, industry, depots, rail yards, ports) are known to both sides at the outbreak from peacetime intelligence; the picture tracks what moves and what was hit (decision 132).

- **Reconnaissance satellites** fly fixed circular orbits modeled on Cold War systems (film-return and digital imaging, radar and ELINT ocean reconnaissance, late radar imaging). Optical imaging needs daylight and clear sky. A contact carries its observation time but reaches the picture only after its delivery delay, from minutes for ocean reconnaissance to days for film return.
- **Ground radar and AWACS** give the air picture within the radar horizon and terrain masking. The air picture also drives the Pact's ground-controlled interception.
- **Reconnaissance missions** refresh contacts along their routes.

Pass times are analytic and detection rolls come from per-side forks, so the picture is identical at any time acceleration. An overlay shows the viewing side's contacts and satellite tracks.

## Mission Search and Jump-In

Mission search is the player's main interface to the war. A panel lists the ongoing and upcoming missions of the player's side, with filters for mission type, aircraft, theater, current phase, threat level, and time until action ("on target in under 5 minutes"). Selecting a mission focuses the map on it and draws its route. *Follow* carries the camera through the transition into the simulation view, with a chase camera behind one of its aircraft; *Jump in* (roadmap R2.5) gives the player that aircraft's controls in the same chase view, so only the source of the inputs changes. Returning to the map lands the strategic camera above the flight's position.

- **Jump-in window.** A mission accepts the player from climb-out until approach. Takeoff and landing stay with the AI, so joining puts the player in the air with the package already formed.
- **Helicopter missions** (decision 149). For helicopters, landing is the mission: inserting troops, delivering a sling load, reaching a forward arming and refueling point. Their jump-in window extends over their ground phases, and the player may fly every takeoff and landing.
- **Handback.** Leaving an aircraft returns it to the AI with its current orders, at any phase. While the player flies a flight, the campaign holds its aggregate resolution and its phase events; at the handback the worker rebuilds its route from where the flight is, carrying on the mission from its phase there, and logs the aircraft full fidelity lost (decision 257), the session (which aircraft the player flew, from when, what it lost, the fuel left and the stores expended) and what each surviving aircraft has expended, which the next promotion starts from (decision 275).
- **Subscriptions.** The player can subscribe to a query, such as "next attack helicopter CAS in corridor 2" or "any strike on our airbases". When a matching mission reaches the chosen phase, auto-slowdown drops time to 1× and offers the jump. Subscriptions combine time acceleration and search into one loop: accelerate, get notified, jump in.

The tech demo (roadmap R2) includes the panel, subscriptions, following, taking over and handing back. In the demo, fixed-wing jump-in runs from climb-out to approach, since the AI cannot yet taxi or land through the flight model (decision 231).

*Follow* exists (decision 252): from a row of mission search, from the map's selection panel, or from a subscription's offer, once the flight has started. It sets the clock to 1× and cuts to the chase camera behind the flight's lead, which settles in from further back; N steps through the flight's aircraft, and Escape, M or the bar's button returns to the map above the flight. The followed aircraft fly their analytic routes in formation, drawn over the drawn ground; the HUD and the aircraft's own displays show what its route knows, read-only, and other flights near the camera are drawn too. A followed flight whose type has fixed-wing flight data (the F-21 today) flies at full fidelity inside its jump-in window while the clock runs at the flight cap (8×) or slower: AI pilots fly its aircraft through the flight model over the drawn ground with the airfields' grading, in the campaign's wind and gusts (decision 278), the lead keeping station on its route as the view draws it, and the aircraft, the HUD and the displays are drawn from the airframes; at the approach it returns to its analytic route, the drawn aircraft easing back onto it (decision 272). Inside the window T or the bar's button takes over the followed aircraft: the jet flies with Free Flight's bindings, mouse aim and the one mouse capture (Tab), the wingmen keep formation on the player, the displays take their bindings, and Space and the digits leave the clock for the jet and the displays. T again, the map button or a crash (after three seconds) hands it back to the AI; the worker gets the take-over and hand-back inputs, and a held flight is not ended at its old route's end (decision 273). A spectator sees the HUD alone by default; V steps to the HUD with the displays and to a clean view (decision 253). Aircraft near an airbase are drawn on its sited field, carrier groups near the camera are drawn on their tracks, and a carrier's flight stands on its deck (decision 255). The transition from the orbit down into the aircraft (below) is a stretch goal for R6.6.

## Flying

*Specified in `docs/proposals/0002-third-person-flight.md` (decision 94), for helicopters `docs/proposals/0004-helicopter-flight.md` (decision 149), and for the displays, status strip, master modes and crews `docs/proposals/0006-displays-and-crew.md` (decision 189; the plan is `docs/plans/displays.md`); `docs/visual-style.md`, Part I, covers the controlled aircraft's model and the look of the HUD and displays.*

- **Camera rig.** A chase camera behind and slightly above the aircraft, attached through a spring-damper so it lags behind rolls, pitch changes, and acceleration while keeping the aircraft near the center of the screen. Each aircraft class gets its own offsets and damping. Free look orbits the camera while the right mouse button (or a key) is held and stays where it is left until the player recenters it; the mouse wheel moves the camera closer or farther while looking (decisions 160, 198). A jet's camera looks at a point just ahead of the nose from behind and onto the aircraft itself as the eye orbits away, so free look orbits the aircraft (decision 253). A target camera keeps the aircraft and the selected target in frame, a weapon camera can follow a released missile or bomb, and flyby and cinematic cameras serve transitions, replays, and idle spectating. The camera raycasts against the height map, never clips terrain, and lowers and closes in at low altitude.
- **Flight director and controls.** The flight director is an autopilot that turns a desired direction and speed into control inputs for the flight model. With mouse aim (the default), the mouse steers an aim point in the world and the flight director flies toward it, while the rudder keys stay the pilot's and a held stick key takes its axis from the director (decision 219); with direct control, the keyboard, the captured mouse's offset from the screen's center, or a flight stick drive the control surfaces through a stability augmentation layer (decision 198). AI pilots use the same flight director and the same flight model, so handback only gives the flight director an aim point from the AI's orders instead of from the mouse. A campaign flight's orders are its analytic route: at full fidelity its lead keeps station on the point the route gives for the moment, and its wingmen on their formation slots beside the flown lead, speed first (the climb and the bank give way before the airspeed does); a flight handed back gets a route rebuilt from where it is, which carries on the mission from its phase there (decision 256).
- **Flight model.** Table-driven at medium fidelity: per-type thrust, drag, lift, instantaneous and sustained turn rates, specific excess power, roll rate, and the effects of altitude, wing sweep, and stores, with energy management, stalls, and departures in simplified form. The tables live in the aircraft's JSON bundle next to its geometry, its avionics profile, its air-war entry and its camera rig (`src/data/models/aircraft/`, decisions 232 and 238). Today only the Kestrel has fixed-wing tables, and rotation comes from its fly-by-wire control laws, without departures (decision 134). The ground model is to treat the forest canopy as an obstacle (planned; it does not yet): rotor or airframe below the canopy top inside a closed stand strikes the trees, while hovering beside a tree line is safe (decision 148).
- **Helicopters** (decision 149, proposal 0004). A rotor-disc model of medium fidelity beside the fixed-wing one: cyclic tilts the thrust disc with a flapping lag, hub stiffness (teetering, articulated, hingeless) sets low-g behavior, power required follows the bucket with translational lift and ground effect, a tail rotor of limited authority counters torque, and retreating blade stall, vortex ring state, mast bumping, autorotation and tail rotor loss are modeled hazards. Three control modes feed the same flight director: fly-by-mouse (the default; the mouse's horizontal motion yaws in the hover and banks into a coordinated turn in cruise), mouse aim for gun and rocket runs, and direct control for sticks. Assists toggle one by one under three presets (Assisted, Standard, Expert); switching one off removes protection, never a capability. The helicopter HUD adds hover symbology (a ground-plane velocity vector with an acceleration cue, radar altitude, torque, rotor RPM, landing zone marker, hazard cues), and the helicopter camera rig sits closer and higher and lifts and tilts down as airspeed falls.
- **Allocation.** Every item lives in exactly one place: the **HUD** (spatial and time-critical information used while looking outside), a **display** (sensor pictures, selection, configuration, planning) or the **status strip** (discrete states and warnings). A new item takes its place by that rule (decision 189).
- **HUD.** World-anchored symbols are projected from the scene every frame (flight path marker, boresight, gun pipper, CCIP fall lines, target boxes, waypoint diamonds, seeker circles); screen-anchored instruments sit at fixed positions (airspeed, altitude, and heading tapes, a compact attitude indicator instead of a pitch ladder, Mach, G, the throttle). Warnings that demand an immediate maneuver, such as PULL UP, stay on the HUD.
- **Displays.** Five docked slots with fixed positions and bindings in every aircraft: a large multifunction display lower left and lower right, a small threat display upper right, a small messages display upper left, and the status strip at bottom center. The middle 60% of the screen belongs to the aircraft and the HUD. Each slot is expanded, collapsed to a tab or hidden; slots never move, a pinned slot keeps its format in every mode, and an alert may bring a format forward in its own slot. Every display uses one frame, a square screen with bezel buttons. Its formats are the air-to-air and air-to-ground radars (with the terrain-following scope), stores, a moving map (in the strategic view's illustrated style, or by elevation with a mode relative to the aircraft's altitude), targeting pod, threat warning, comms, engines and fuel, mission, crew, and for helicopters the hook camera and torque and rotor gauges at the end of the strip. A format has the same layout and buttons wherever it exists, and an aircraft has a format only if it carries the system behind it; its avionics profile lists its slots and formats. Datalink tracks from AWACS or ground control appear on the moving map and the radar. The Su-27 analogue alone also shows its radar in the HUD. A followed aircraft's displays show its own formats with its picture, read-only, as its HUD does, when the spectator asks for them (V; decisions 252, 253).
- **Status strip.** Dark in normal flight: items appear only when relevant (parking brake, gear, speedbrake, master arm, afterburner) and warn when a state conflicts with the situation (gear up while slow and low), in green, amber and red with a label and a shape each, and a master caution acknowledges the cautions.
- **Master modes.** Ground, navigation, air-to-air, dogfight, air-to-ground, approach and, for helicopters, hover reconfigure the slots and the strip together, and the HUD's mode symbology (declutter by mode is planned). They follow triggers (weight on wheels, gear down below 3,000 ft, a weapon selected, below 20 kt) or a key; each mode's slot preset can be edited once for every aircraft.
- **Cursor mode.** One key (Tab) captures and frees the mouse in every aircraft (decision 193). The free cursor clicks the bezel buttons, strip items and contacts while the flight director holds the aim point in mouse aim; in direct control the stick centers (decision 198). Keys cover the hot actions a pilot needs without looking (master modes, slot states, radar lock and designation, countermeasures, the crew menu); bezel buttons work by mouse. With a flight stick the mouse stays free.
- **Display models.** Every format draws from a serializable display model, a pure function of the sensor-filtered snapshot and the display state, so the AI crewmate, tests and later companion displays in a second window or on a tablet (P6) work through the same commands as the player. All displays together take under 2 ms of CPU per frame.
- **Sensors.** A sensor model filters simulation state per aircraft: radar by type, mode, range, radar cross-section, aspect, terrain masking, and clutter; RWR; visual detection; datalinks and AWACS with lower precision and a delay; targeting pods and terrain-following radar. Avionics profiles per type set which sensors exist and how good they are. The displays show only this filtered picture, and the AI is to use the same one. Today radar, RWR, visual detection and datalink exist for Free Flight (decision 218); pods, terrain-following radar and the AI's use are planned.
- **Crews.** See *Factions and Sides*. Transports add an airdrop format with a drop zone marker and release cue, and transport helicopters the hook camera format for sling loads.
- **Free Flight** (decision 134). A sandbox outside the campaign: pick a seed and an airbase, take off in the F-21 Kestrel or one of two helicopters (decision 159), fly anywhere and land at any airbase, with no war or weapons; optional scripted traffic and ground radars feed the sensors (decision 218). Time of day and weather are options: the world's own weather or a preset from clear sky to thunderstorm, blizzard, fog and dust storm, with wind and turbulence on the aircraft; the flight is on a day with a full moon (decision 143). It brings a first version of P1 (flight model, control laws, direct control, flight director) and part of P2 (the HUD) forward, adds ground handling and landing gear, and builds them where the campaign will reuse them (roadmap R1.5). Takeoff and landing stay with the AI in the campaign.

## The Ground War

The ground war exists wherever the cast contains a continental front, chokepoint, or island campaign. AI controls it entirely. The player shapes it from the air.

- **Order of battle.** Divisions contain brigades or regiments, which contain battalions. Battalions are the aggregate unit: type (tank, mechanized infantry, motorized infantry, artillery, air defense, logistics), strength, supply, and posture.
- **Aggregate combat.** Lanchester-style attrition each tick, modified by terrain, posture, supply, artillery, and air support. Interdiction cuts supply; CAS reduces strength directly.
- **As built for the demo** (decision 251). Each continental front's start line is cut into segments of about 80 km; the battalions of both sides deploy on them, the attacker massed on its main axes at the corridors and the defender spread. Each game minute every segment fights by Lanchester's square law, the defender helped by the barrier's terrain and prepared positions, the fighting intense where the attacker is on the offensive and quiet elsewhere; an offensive culminates when the attacker there has lost half its strength. Segments move by the force ratio, up to 25 km a day, slowed by terrain and held together, and the front line is the start line moved by them. Supply is the best way by road from a side's depots and rear cities to each segment, cut bridges, detours, damaged depots and roads lowering it. Battalion types are tank, mechanized, artillery, air defense and logistics; motorized infantry and posture beyond attack and defense wait for R3.6. The map shows brigades and regiments as NATO-style symbols at the Region band.
- **Entities.** Inside the promotion bubble, battalions expand into tank and IFV platoons, infantry squads with MANPADS teams, artillery, short-range air defense, and logistics columns on roads. Air defense entities matter most, since they decide whether an attack run survives.
- **Line of sight.** Attack helicopters fight by terrain masking and forest cover, so line-of-sight queries are to use the level-7 tiles (about 48 m) and the canopy height field, batched on the GPU within the bubble. Today line of sight is a CPU march over the ground only (decision 218). A closed forest is a volume from the ground to its canopy top, and for any sight line from or to an aircraft it is opaque, edges included; how far ground units see into and out of a forest edge is settled with the ground entities (decision 148).

The tech demo (roadmap R2) runs battalions in aggregate, shown as unit symbols on a moving front, with minimal entity promotion: near the player a battalion spawns a handful of representative vehicles (tanks, infantry fighting vehicles, trucks, a short-range air defense vehicle) that can be targeted, and their losses fold back into its strength. Full entity behavior follows in R3.

## Naval Forces

Convoys, carrier groups, and amphibious groups exist wherever the cast contains a sea lane, chokepoint, or island campaign. AI controls them. They are targets and protectees for air missions, and convoy arrivals deliver reinforcements and supplies to the receiving theater. Casting assigns carrier groups to sides, and their air wings fly ATO missions (see *Air Tasking*). A group starts the war on station, patrolling a stretch of a sea lane in its theater (decision 132). Proposal 0008 adds surface action groups and abstract submarines. Naval groups move on sea lanes and fight with aggregate resolvers, and the player never commands ships; air missions meet them through anti-shipping strikes, maritime patrol, convoy escort and carrier operations. Carrier groups patrol today; the naval war arrives in roadmap phase R4.

## Rendering

The two views share world data, the simulation, and camera-relative rendering, and differ in everything they draw. `docs/visual-style.md` specifies both.

### Strategic View

*Specified in `docs/visual-style.md`, Part II.* An orbit camera from about 3,000 km down to a zoom floor of about 100 km, pitching from straight down at globe scale to about 50° at the floor. Content fades in and out by zoom band (globe, theater, region). A cube-sphere relief mesh with 4× exaggeration and a cartographic hillshade carries painted biomes with procedural patterns, banded water with coastal lines, low-poly glyphs for mountains, forests, and cities, a road hierarchy with casing, hierarchical labels with collision handling, and the military overlays: territory, the front line, defensive lines, named corridors, sea lanes, chokepoints, air defense rings, unit symbols, and flights. Zone names label the largest regions, spaced apart, at the Theater and Region bands (decision 220), and natural wonders are labeled (decision 204; their own glyph is planned). Day and night, seasons, and the live weather are always shown in stylized form: a terminator with a legible night side and settlement lights, and soft cloud shapes from the weather field. Individual trees and buildings never appear.

### Simulation View

*Specified in `docs/visual-style.md`, Part I.* Faceted, flat-shaded terrain at true scale from a quadtree limited to the horizon (about 200 km at 20 km altitude, farther for peaks beyond it), forests as a raised, faceted canopy with the tree kit in front of it near the camera (decision 148), box-building settlements and the base kit, roads and railways drawn in the terrain with their embankments and cuttings, bridges and tunnel portals from a kit (decision 161), procedurally built models, the physical sky with sun, moon and stars and aerial perspective, ocean, terrain shadows, volumetric clouds, fog and dust from the live weather, rain and snow with lightning and thunder, and the full post-processing chain. A governor lowers the atmospheric quality before the frame rate falls under its target (decision 143). Each zone has its own look: a shift of its biome colors, haze, sky grading, sun warmth, cloud regime and ambient particles, blended across boundaries without a barrier; landform props stand where the height field cannot reach (decision 204). The aircraft the player controls or follows gets a higher detail level with animated parts, and the HUD, the docked displays and the status strip draw as 2D overlays.

### Transition

Selecting a flight and choosing *Follow* or *Jump in* eases the strategic camera toward the flight down to the zoom floor. A quick dissolve through a cloud layer hides the switch, and the simulation view opens with a chase camera behind the aircraft. Returning reverses the sequence. The whole transition lasts under two seconds. Until R6.6 the switch is a cut with a chase-camera settle (owner decision 12, decision 252).

## Open Questions

- **Aggressor.** Should the Pact always attack, or should casting occasionally reverse the roles when the geography favors an Alliance offensive?
- **Thresholds.** What are the right fit thresholds for each theater? The seed browser (S3) tells us how often each theater appears.
- **Range factor.** Does the per-world range factor feel right? We should fly representative sorties once following works (roadmap R2.4). The first seed-browser run gives 1.0 for almost every seed, because fronts are deeper than a medium strike radius.
- **Zoom floor.** Is about 100 km altitude close enough, or should the floor go lower at the cost of more glyph and pattern detail?
- **Vertical exaggeration.** A fixed 4× on the map, or an exaggeration that increases as the camera pulls out?
- **Naming flavor.** How many cultural naming regions per world, and should they follow faction territory or landmasses?
- **Language of labels.** Should Pact-side names appear in a transliterated style, with an optional Cyrillic-like display font as flavor?
- **Strategic animation.** How much motion belongs on the map: moving flight icons only, or also animated front pushes, smoke over battles, and convoy wakes?
- **Free look in combat.** Should free look be limited while the player is in combat, so the chase view gives less of an advantage, or left unrestricted?
- **HUD color.** Green for both factions, or green for the Alliance and a different hue for the Pact, as a subtle cue for the side being flown?
- **Voice.** Should crew callouts and messages use synthesized speech from the start of the crews (roadmap R4.5), or text first? (The plan recommends text first; decision 189.)
- **Single-seat help.** Should single-seat aircraft get a light automation assistant (for example, the radar's mode following the master mode), or stay fully manual? (The plan recommends avionics assists, each a toggle.)
- **Clean mode.** Should a mode exist that hides all overlays for screenshots and spectating?
- **Wonders in the war.** Are natural wonders strike-free landmarks, or may a war fought around them leave its mark (a bridge over a gorge, a base on a salt flat)? (Decision 204.)
- **Zone groups.** At 16–63 km (decision 205) a world has a few thousand zones, and the map names only the largest, spaced apart (decision 220): should zones also group into larger named regions?
- **Transport helicopters' fire support.** Should the Black Hawk, Huey and Mi-8 analogues also fly fire support (door guns, rocket pods), or only carry troops and cargo? (Decision 151.)
